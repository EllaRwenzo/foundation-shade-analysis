What the data shows

  • Every brand reaches the deep end, but not equally far. Each brand's deepest shade lands in the Deep band. It ranges from L* {{DEEPEST_L}} ({{DEEPEST_BRAND}}) to L* {{SHALLOW_L}} ({{SHALLOW_BRAND}}).
  • Most shades are light. {{SHARE_LIGHTER}} of all shades are in the lighter half of the scale. The share in the deeper half runs from {{LOW_SHARE}} ({{LOW_BRAND}}) to {{TOP_SHARE}} ({{TOP_BRAND}}), and no brand reaches 50%.
  • The pattern repeats inside each product. In {{N_UNBALANCED}} of {{N_PRODUCTS}} products, fewer than half the shades sit in the deeper half of that product's own range. The typical product puts {{MEDIAN_OWN_HALF}} there.
  • Deep shades are spaced further apart. The typical gap between neighboring shades is {{GAP_LIGHT}} lightness points in a product's lighter half and {{GAP_DEEP}} in its deeper half. That held in {{N_WIDER}} of {{N_TESTED}} products (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001).

Collecting. For each brand I took every foundation, skin tint, CC cream and glow filter in the Foundation section of its US website, and every shade listed, including sold-out ones. Seven brands paint their swatches with a color code, which I recorded exactly. Charlotte Tilbury, Rare Beauty and Makeup by Mario use small photos, so I averaged the middle of each photo after dropping the brightest and darkest quarter of pixels. Three products the brands are clearing out were left out.

Measuring lightness. CIELAB L* is the standard measure of how light a color looks, from 0 (black) to 100 (white). It weights green light more than blue, the way our eyes do:

Y = 0.2126·R + 0.7152·G + 0.0722·Bbrightness, after R, G and B are converted to linear lightL* = 116 · Y1/3 − 16lightness on a 0–100 scale

Bands. I split L* 15 to 95 into four equal 20-point bands: Light (75+), Medium (55–75), Tan (35–55) and Deep (below 35). That span matches the ten tones of the Monk Skin Tone Scale, shown as the last row of the chart above.

Testing. For each product I compared the typical gap between neighboring shades in its lighter half and its deeper half. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test fits this kind of paired, skewed data. Everything is in R: dplyr for cleaning, farver to check the color math, and ggplot2 for the charts.

What it might mean

  • A person with deep skin has fewer shades to choose from and bigger jumps between them, so the closest shade is more likely to be a visible mismatch.
  • A long shade list on the box is not enough to judge inclusivity. How the shades are spaced matters as much as how many there are.
  • Brands may be placing shades where they expect the most sales. This data shows how shades are spread, not why.

Limits of this data

  • Screen colors, not makeup. Swatches are digital images, and brands don't calibrate them against each other. Comparisons inside a brand are more reliable than between brands.
  • Swatches are approximate. {{N_REPEATED}} pairs of shades share one swatch color, and in {{N_ORDER_FLAGS}} spots a higher-numbered shade has a clearly lighter swatch than the one before it.
  • Lightness only. Undertone matters too. The a* and b* values are saved for a follow-up.
  • One day, ten brands. Lineups change, and these brands are not a random sample of the market.
